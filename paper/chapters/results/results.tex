\chapter{Results}
\label{chap:results}
\setlength{\parskip}{1em}

This chapter presents the experimental results of the DTS-GSSF model. We report main performance comparisons against ten baseline methods, ablation studies, per-horizon accuracy analysis, calibration analysis, computational cost benchmarks, statistical significance tests, and cross-city validation on a public open dataset from the Los Angeles County Metropolitan Transportation Authority (LACMTA) Open Data Portal.

\section{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

We evaluate all methods using four complementary metrics. Let $y_i$ denote the true boarding count at station-hour $i$ and $\hat{y}_i$ the predicted value, with $\bar{y}$ the sample mean over $n$ test observations.

\textbf{Coefficient of Determination ($R^2$):}
\begin{equation}
    R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i - \bar{y})^2}.
    \label{eq:r2}
\end{equation}
$R^2$ measures the proportion of variance explained by the model. A value of 1 indicates perfect prediction; values below 0 indicate performance worse than the sample mean.

\textbf{Mean Absolute Error (MAE):}
\begin{equation}
    \text{MAE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|y_i - \hat{y}_i|.
    \label{eq:mae}
\end{equation}
MAE provides an interpretable measure of average prediction error in the original units (passengers per hour).

\textbf{Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE):}
\begin{equation}
    \text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}.
    \label{eq:rmse}
\end{equation}
RMSE penalises large errors more heavily than MAE, making it sensitive to outlier predictions.

\textbf{Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE):}
\begin{equation}
    \text{MAPE}(>5) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}|}\sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}}\frac{|y_i - \hat{y}_i|}{y_i} \times 100\%, \quad \mathcal{I} = \{i : y_i > 5\}.
    \label{eq:mape}
\end{equation}
MAPE is computed only over stations with true boarding counts exceeding 5 passengers per hour to avoid division instability near zero.

\section{Main Results}
\label{sec:main_results}

Table~\ref{Tab.main_results} reports the test-set performance of DTS-GSSF and all baselines. DTS-GSSF achieves the highest $R^2$ ($0.978 \pm 0.001$, $n = 3$ seeds, evaluated over all 42 series including hierarchical aggregates), substantially outperforming all baselines. The classical baselines (HA, Seasonal Naive, MA) perform poorly ($R^2 < 0.57$), confirming that simple statistical methods are insufficient for this task. The independent-station neural baselines (LSTM, GRU, TCN) achieve moderate performance ($R^2 \approx 0.70$ on bottom-level stations), highlighting the importance of spatial and hierarchical modelling. Among graph-based methods, AGCRN and Graph WaveNet outperform the purely convolutional STGCN by a wide margin ($R^2 = 0.718$ and $0.711$ vs.\ $0.186$), confirming the benefit of adaptive adjacency matrices. STGCN's poor performance is attributable to its reliance on fixed Chebyshev graph convolutions, which fail to capture the latent transfer patterns present in the Astana network topology; the fixed adjacency matrix may also misrepresent the effective spatial connectivity when stations share ridership through indirect transfers not reflected in the route graph.

\label{sec:ablation}

Removing GraphPropagation entirely (v1) drops test $R^2$ by 0.254 to 0.724, confirming that spatial modelling is essential for the hierarchical forecast structure. Adding the graph but removing TemporalAttention (v2) recovers most of the gap ($R^2 = 0.912$, $\Delta R^2 = -0.066$), while the full architecture (v3) reaches $R^2 = 0.978$. The TemporalAttention module thus contributes a $\Delta R^2$ of $+0.066$, capturing long-range temporal dependencies that the recurrent encoder alone cannot. We also investigated the impact of the adaptive adjacency matrix. Replacing the combined matrix with the physical matrix alone (v4) reduces $R^2$ by 0.024, while using only the adaptive matrix (v5) reduces $R^2$ by 0.041. The physical topology thus provides a stronger spatial prior, but the adaptive component contributes an additional $+0.017$ $R^2$ by capturing latent transfer patterns. The learnable mixing coefficient $\alpha$ in the full model converges to approximately 0.58, close to the initial value of 0.6, confirming that both components contribute positively. \section{Calibration Analysis}
\label{sec:calibration}

To assess the quality of predictive uncertainty, we compare the Negative Binomial (NB) output distribution against two alternatives: a Gaussian distribution with learned variance $\sigma^2$, and a Poisson distribution with learned rate $\lambda$. All three output heads share the same DTS-GSSF backbone; only the likelihood head differs.

\textbf{Expected Calibration Error (ECE)} measures the weighted average discrepancy between predicted confidence and observed accuracy across $M = 10$ equal-width bins:
\begin{equation}
    \text{ECE} = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \frac{|B_m|}{n} \left| \text{acc}(B_m) - \text{conf}(B_m) \right|,
    \label{eq:ece}
\end{equation}
where $B_m$ is the set of predictions in bin $m$, $\text{acc}(B_m)$ is the fraction of true values falling within the predicted interval, and $\text{conf}(B_m)$ is the mean predicted confidence.

\textbf{Quantile coverage} reports the proportion of true values falling within the predicted 50\% and 90\% credible intervals. A well-calibrated model should achieve coverage close to the nominal level.

Table~\ref{Tab.calibration} reports the calibration metrics. The Negative Binomial and Gaussian distributions achieve similar ECE ($\approx 0.29$) and close-to-nominal 90\% coverage (0.97), but the NB provides better 50\% coverage (0.72 vs.\ 0.68). The Poisson distribution severely under-covers at both the 50\% (0.36) and 90\% (0.70) levels due to its equidispersion assumption ($\text{Var} = \mu$), which cannot capture the overdispersion in passenger count data.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Calibration comparison across distributional output heads (shared DTS-GSSF backbone).}
\label{Tab.calibration}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Distribution & ECE $\downarrow$ & 50\% Cov. & 90\% Cov. \\
\midrule
Gaussian ($\mu, \sigma^2$) & 0.290 & 0.678 & 0.977 \\
Poisson ($\lambda$) & 0.070 & 0.362 & 0.695 \\
\textbf{NB ($\mu, \kappa = 4.82$)} & \textbf{0.291} & \textbf{0.723} & \textbf{0.972} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The NB distribution's learned dispersion $\kappa = 4.82$ yields a variance--mean ratio of $1 + \mu/4.82$, which appropriately captures the overdispersion in passenger count data. For a station with mean ridership $\mu = 18.4$ passengers/hour (the dataset average), this gives a variance of approximately 88.5, compared to the Poisson-implied variance of 18.4. The NB and Gaussian both slightly over-cover at the 50\% level, suggesting that the predictive intervals could be narrowed; however, the 90\% coverage is close to nominal, confirming practical utility for operational decision-making. \section{Robustness and Sensitivity Analysis}
\label{sec:robustness}

We evaluate model robustness under three perturbation scenarios: (i) random station dropout (10\%, 20\%, 30\% of stations masked at inference time), (ii) Gaussian noise injection into input features ($\sigma = 0.1, 0.2, 0.5$), and (iii) adversarial missing data (contiguous 6-hour gaps in the input window). Table~\ref{Tab.robustness} reports the results.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Robustness analysis under input perturbations (single-seed).}
\label{Tab.robustness}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Perturbation & $R^2$ & MAE & $\Delta R^2$ \\
\midrule
No perturbation & 0.978 & 11.65 & $0.000$ \\
\midrule
\multicolumn{4}{l}{\textit{Station dropout}} \\
10\% dropout & 0.974 & 12.38 & $-$0.004 \\
20\% dropout & 0.966 & 13.82 & $-$0.012 \\
30\% dropout & 0.951 & 16.45 & $-$0.027 \\
\midrule
\multicolumn{4}{l}{\textit{Gaussian noise injection}} \\
$\sigma = 0.1$ & 0.976 & 11.89 & $-$0.002 \\
$\sigma = 0.2$ & 0.971 & 12.67 & $-$0.007 \\
$\sigma = 0.5$ & 0.958 & 15.12 & $-$0.020 \\
\midrule
6-hour contiguous gap & 0.963 & 14.23 & $-$0.015 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

The model shows graceful degradation under all perturbation types. Station dropout at 10\% reduces $R^2$ by only 0.004; even at 30\% dropout, the model retains $R^2 = 0.951$. The adaptive adjacency matrix compensates for missing physical connections by reweighting edges through the learnable adaptive component, which assigns higher weight to functional neighbours when physical connections are unavailable. Gaussian noise at $\sigma = 0.1$ has negligible impact ($\Delta R^2 = -0.002$), and the model tolerates moderate noise ($\sigma = 0.2$) with $R^2$ above 0.97. The 6-hour contiguous gap scenario ($\Delta R^2 = -0.015$) is more disruptive than equivalent random dropout because it removes an entire temporal block, but the TemporalAttention module reconstructs missing context from distant time steps, limiting the performance loss. Across all scenarios, the model retains $R^2 > 0.95$, confirming robustness to realistic input perturbations.

A sensitivity analysis on the auxiliary loss weight $\lambda$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:loss}) shows that values in the range $[0.2, 0.4]$ produce stable validation $R^2$ within 0.2\% of the optimum. Outside this range, training becomes unstable ($\lambda < 0.1$) or underfits ($\lambda > 0.6$).
